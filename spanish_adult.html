Pronunciación

Spanish spelling is far more consistent than English — once you know the rules, you can read almost any word correctly on sight.

🇵🇱 Spanish vowels are short, pure, and always sound the same — much like Polish vowels. There's no equivalent of English's sliding "long a" in cake or the schwa in about.

Género y artículos — Gender & articles

Every Spanish noun is grammatically masculine or feminine — even for objects with no natural gender.

🇵🇱 This will feel familiar: Polish also assigns gender to every noun (stół is masculine, lampa feminine), though the endings that signal it are different.
🇬🇧 English dropped grammatical gender centuries ago — "the table" and "the lamp" use the same article. In Spanish they don't.

Spotting the gender

Making plurals

Ends in a vowel → add -s (casa → casas ). Ends in a consonant → add -es (ciudad → ciudades ).

Pronombres — Subject pronouns

🇵🇱 Spanish and Polish are both "pro-drop" languages: the verb ending already tells you who's acting, so the pronoun is often left out. Hablo español already means "I speak Spanish" — no yo needed, just as Mówię po hiszpańsku needs no ja.
🇬🇧 English can't drop its pronouns — "Speak Spanish" on its own doesn't tell you who.

Ser vs. Estar — two verbs for "to be"

English "to be" and Polish "być" each do one job. Spanish splits it in two.

vosotrossois estáis

Use ser for…Use estar for…
Soy profesor.
(I am a teacher — profession)		Estoy cansado.
(I am tired — temporary state)

(He is tall — inherent trait)		Está en casa.

Es de Polonia.
(He is from Poland — origin)		Está enfermo.
(He is sick — changeable condition)
Same adjective, different meaning: Es aburrido = "He is boring" (a trait). Está aburrido = "He is bored" (right now). This distinction doesn't exist in English or Polish, so it takes deliberate practice.

Two constructions worth learning immediately

tener que + infinitive to have to do something — Tengo que estudiar. (I have to study.)
ir a + infinitive going to do something (near future) — Voy a comer. (I'm going to eat.)

Adjetivos — Adjective agreement & position

🇬🇧🇵🇱 In English and Polish, adjectives normally come before the noun: "red car" / "czerwony samochód". In Spanish, most descriptive adjectives come after: coche rojo.

Adjectives also change to match the noun's gender and number:

masculinefeminine
singularcoche rojo casa roja
pluralcoches rojos casas rojas
Adjectives ending in -e or a consonant don't change for gender, only number: grande → grandes , fácil → fáciles .

Only nuestro and vuestro change for gender (nuestra casa ); the others just add -s for plural.

Frases y saludos — Everyday phrases

🇵🇱 Spanish has a formal/informal "you" split, just like Polish ty vs. pan/pani: use tú with friends, family, and children, and usted with strangers, elders, or in business settings.
🇬🇧 English lost this distinction entirely — "you" covers both, so this is one place your English instincts won't help.

Números especiales — how compound numbers are built

From 31 onward, Spanish snaps a tens-word and a units-word together with y ("and") — two separate words:

cuarenta+ y +cuatro= cuarenta y cuatro (44)
ochenta+ y +siete= ochenta y siete (87)
noventa+ y +nueve= noventa y nueve (99)

But 16–29 fuse into a single word instead — a leftover of "diez y seis" (10 and 6) contracting over time into dieciséis . Same for veintidós (22), veintiocho (28). Neither English nor Polish does this — "twenty-two" and "dwadzieścia dwa" both stay as two clean words at every stage.

One more quirk: veintiuno (21) shortens to veintiún right before a noun — veintiún años (21 years) — the same way uno shortens to un before a masculine noun.

La hora — Telling time

Es la una. It's one o'clock.
Son las tres. It's three o'clock.
Son las tres y media. It's half past three.
Son las cuatro menos cuarto. It's a quarter to four.
Note es la una (singular, only for 1:00) versus son las… (plural, every other hour) — the verb agrees with the number of hours. Minutes past the half hour are usually counted down from the next hour with menos ("minus") rather than up from the current one — so 2:35 is "three minus twenty-five", not "two and thirty-five" (though you'll hear the simpler additive form too, especially in digital contexts).

Fechas — Dates

Spanish reads a date as "the [day] of [month] of [year]" — and reads the year as one whole number, unlike English, which often splits it in two ("nineteen ninety-eight").

WrittenSpoken
7 de enero de 1998
1 de mayo de 2000
21 de agosto de 1500
Hoy — today
The 1st of the month is the one exception to using plain cardinal numbers: el primero de enero (in Spain you'll also hear el uno de enero ). Every other day just uses the normal number: el veintiuno de agosto .

Días y meses — Days & months

Days and months are not capitalized in Spanish, unlike English: lunes , enero .
